# The World of the New Testament

## A changing political world

The centuries between the principal Old Testament narratives and Jesus' ministry include Persian, Greek, and Roman rule. Alexander's conquests spread Greek influence; later rival kingdoms competed for the region. The Maccabean revolt and the Hasmonean dynasty preceded Roman domination, marked by Pompey's intervention in Jerusalem in 63 BC.

This is orientation, not a complete chronology. The Maccabean family was priestly, although not of the traditional Zadokite high-priestly line. Avoid describing them as simply having no priestly ancestry.

Hebrew, Aramaic, Greek, and Latin had different roles. Greek was widely used across the eastern Mediterranean; the New Testament books were written in Greek. These overlapping languages remind us that the world of Jesus was not culturally uniform.

## Jewish life was diverse

Jesus, His first disciples, and many early believers were Jewish. Temple worship, scripture, prayer, covenant identity, and questions of faithful living shaped their world.

- **Pharisees:** A movement concerned with faithful interpretation and practice of the law, including received traditions. The Gospels portray both disputes and individual encounters; the name is not a synonym for hypocrisy.
- **Sadducees:** Associated especially with priestly and aristocratic leadership. Their views differed from the Pharisees on matters such as resurrection; see Acts 23:6-8.
- **Essenes:** Groups associated with distinctive communal practices and separation. Their relationship to the Dead Sea Scrolls is a subject of historical study, not an identification supplied by the Gospels.
- **Zeal and resistance:** Opposition to Roman rule took different forms. Be cautious about treating every first-century expression of zeal as membership in one organized party throughout the period.

[Read Acts 23:6-8](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/acts/23?lang=eng&id=p6-p8#p6)

Not everyone belonged to a named group, and people within a group were not identical. The Gospels' controversies should not become contempt for Jewish people then or now. Roman authority, local leadership, ordinary families, and differing social circumstances all belong in the setting.

## Read the person, not just the label

Ask who is speaking, what they want, and what the account actually records. A Pharisee may ask a sincere question; a Roman official may show faith or exercise injustice. No group label supplies every motive.

Historical context can illuminate a passage, but a confident-sounding reconstruction is not the same as evidence. Distinguish explicit statements from plausible background and from an illustration created for teaching.

## Questions to sit with

- How might political power, language, and religious tradition shape the questions people ask Jesus?
- What assumption about a named group might you need to reconsider?
- How can we read a conflict honestly without generalizing its blame to an entire people?
