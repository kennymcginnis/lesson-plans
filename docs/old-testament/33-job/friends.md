# Job 2; 12-14; 21-24 - Presence before Explanation

## They came to comfort him

Eliphaz, Bildad, and Zophar come to mourn with Job. At first they weep, sit on the ground, and say nothing because his grief is so great.

> "None spake a word unto him: for they saw that his grief was very great."

Job 2:13, KJV excerpt.

[Read Job 2:11-13](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/job/2?lang=eng&id=p11-p13#p11)

Their presence begins with recognizing pain rather than explaining it. This can be read alongside Mosiah 18:8-9's invitation to mourn with those who mourn and comfort those who need comfort. It is a thematic connection, not a direct quotation of Job in the Book of Mormon.

[Read Mosiah 18:8-9](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/bofm/mosiah/18?lang=eng&id=p8-p9#p8)

Silence is not always enough, and practical help may be needed. Ask what would help rather than assume that either talking or remaining silent is always the right response.

## When explanation becomes accusation

As the dialogue develops, the friends defend an increasingly rigid account of justice: if Job suffers this much, he must have done something to deserve it. Eliphaz's accusations in chapter 22 illustrate the danger of treating a theory as evidence about a person.

[Read Job 22:1-11](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/job/22?lang=eng&id=p1-p11#p1)

They are trying to defend God, but they do so by making unsupported claims about Job. A religious explanation can cause further harm when it refuses to listen to the person before us.

## Hear Job's answers

| Reading | What to notice |
| --- | --- |
| Job 12-14 | Job insists he also has understanding, challenges the friends' counsel, and longs to bring his case to God. His reflections on fragile human life give the argument its personal urgency. |
| Job 21 | Job describes wicked people who appear to prosper, challenging an immediate reward-and-punishment formula. |
| Job 22 | Eliphaz intensifies the accusations, illustrating how confidence can outrun knowledge. |
| Job 23-24 | Job longs to find God and describes oppression and injustice that are not immediately put right. |

[Read Job 13:1-5, 15-18](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/job/13?lang=eng&id=p1-p5,p15-p18#p1)

[Read Job 21:7-16](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/job/21?lang=eng&id=p7-p16#p7)

[Read Job 23:1-10](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/job/23?lang=eng&id=p1-p10#p1)

Job's protest is not the same as indifference to God. He wants to speak with Him. Some of his conclusions will be challenged, but the book does not solve the problem by deciding his original integrity was a fraud.

## Practice a response that does not pretend to know

Consider a hypothetical friend who says, "I tried to do what was right, and everything still fell apart." Instead of identifying a supposed cause, try acknowledging the loss and offering a concrete form of care.

Possible responses include asking whether they want company, help with a task, or someone to listen. Avoid promising that a lesson learned or a future blessing will make the loss no longer matter.

## Questions to sit with

- What do the friends do well before their arguments begin?
- When can a desire to defend God keep us from listening?
- What is the difference between a belief about justice and evidence about someone's wrongdoing?
- What could you offer a hurting person without needing to explain their suffering?
