# Job 19 - My Redeemer Liveth

## Hope spoken from distress

Before the familiar verses, Job describes abandonment and asks his friends for pity. He wants his words preserved permanently. Read that setting before treating his declaration as a detached statement of certainty.

[Read Job 19:13-27](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/job/19?lang=eng&id=p13-p27#p13)

> "For I know that my redeemer liveth, and that he shall stand at the latter day upon the earth:"

Job 19:25, KJV.

> "And though after my skin worms destroy this body, yet in my flesh shall I see God:"

Job 19:26, KJV.

Job's hope is not that his friends will eventually find a persuasive explanation. He appeals beyond their verdict toward vindication and an encounter with God.

## What does Redeemer mean here?

The language of a redeemer can carry the sense of a defender or one who acts to reclaim, restore, or vindicate. The identity of the figure Job has in view and the precise wording of verses 25-27 are subjects of interpretation; translations differ, particularly over verse 26.

**Christian devotional reading:** Christians, including Latter-day Saints, hear these verses as a witness of the living Redeemer and hope in resurrection. Read that connection alongside the New Testament's explicit testimony of Jesus Christ's Resurrection, rather than claiming that every question about the Hebrew passage is settled by one English rendering.

[Read 1 Corinthians 15:20-22](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/1-cor/15?lang=eng&id=p20-p22#p20)

The promise is not that every possession or relationship lost in mortality will be returned in the same form before death. Our Redeemer's work is larger than a simple reversal of a difficult month or year.

## A companion word from Liberty Jail

D&C 121:7-10 compares Joseph Smith's situation with Job, specifically mentioning friends who do not accuse Joseph as Job's friends accused him. D&C 122:7-9 then directs attention toward Christ's descent below all things and God's continuing presence.

[Read D&C 121:7-10](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/dc-testament/dc/121?lang=eng&id=p7-p10#p7)

[Read D&C 122:7-9](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/dc-testament/dc/122?lang=eng&id=p7-p9#p7)

These passages offer assurance in a specific setting of injustice and imprisonment. They do not require us to call abuse good, refuse lawful protection, or assume we know why another person suffers. God can redeem experience without our justifying the actions that harmed someone.

## Let the hymn become a prayer

The hymn *I Know That My Redeemer Lives* expresses confidence in Christ's living ministry: His love, intercession, sustaining care, and willingness to hear our complaint. Consider its brief line, "He lives to hear my soul's complaint."

That thought allows lament within testimony. We can turn toward the Savior with grief and questions still present, rather than wait until our feelings resemble an idealized version of faith.

## Questions to sit with

- How does Job's request for pity affect the way you hear his declaration of hope?
- What is the difference between trusting a Redeemer and predicting the exact restoration we want?
- How does the Liberty Jail connection keep attention on Christ rather than on our own endurance alone?
- What complaint or hope could you bring honestly into prayer?
