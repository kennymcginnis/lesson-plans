# Understand, Identify, Apply

## Move from wanting to study to knowing how

An unfamiliar prophetic book can leave us knowing that scripture matters but unsure how to read it fruitfully. It is like a coach telling a team to play better without helping them practice a skill.

Try three steps: understand the passage, identify what it teaches, and apply it responsibly. This is a useful study method, not the only valid approach to scripture.

## Understand the context and content

Ask who is speaking, who is addressed, and what situation is in view. Then attend to the actual words: is this a complaint, a warning, a command, a promise, or a song?

- **Nahum:** The opening identifies Nineveh as the subject. The book announces judgment against an oppressive imperial power and speaks of relief for Judah.
- **Habakkuk:** A prophet questions violence and injustice, receives troubling answers, waits for further understanding, and ends in prayer.
- **Zephaniah:** The opening places the prophet in the days of King Josiah. Warnings about Judah and the nations lead into promises of restoration.

The movement from Assyrian dominance toward Babylonian power helps explain the anxieties behind these books. Nineveh was an Assyrian capital in what is now Iraq; Judah lived amid the ambitions of larger powers. Keep historical context as an aid to reading, not a substitute for the text or an excuse to stereotype modern peoples.

## Identify the teaching

Distinguish a truth stated in the passage from a principle you infer from it. Both can be useful, but they should not be presented as the same kind of claim.

In Nahum 1:7, the Lord is explicitly called good and a stronghold in trouble. A possible application is that we can turn to Him during uncertainty. A promise that no faithful person will experience harm goes beyond the verse, which speaks of refuge in a day of trouble rather than the absence of trouble.

[Read Nahum 1:7](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/nahum/1?lang=eng&id=p7#p7)

Use surrounding verses, other scriptures, and trustworthy study resources to test an interpretation. Personal insight can deepen discipleship without becoming a declaration of doctrine for everyone else. Avoid building conclusions on an uncertain word origin or an isolated phrase.

## Apply it to an actual choice

Ask what the passage invites you to trust, reconsider, or do. Be specific enough to act: pray honestly about a concern, keep a commitment while waiting, change a hurtful pattern of speech, or help someone who feels excluded.

An application should fit both the passage and the teachings of Jesus Christ. A prophecy of judgment is not permission for us to threaten people. A promise of future restoration is not a reason to ignore suffering today.

## Practice together

Read Zephaniah 3:17. First name what the verse says about the Lord. Then identify what it might teach about His relationship with His people. Finally, choose one response that does not require assuming every hardship has already ended.

## Questions to sit with

- Which of the three steps do you tend to skip?
- What changes when we ask who originally heard a promise?
- How can we distinguish a meaningful application from a claim the passage does not make?
- What small study habit would make an unfamiliar chapter less intimidating?
