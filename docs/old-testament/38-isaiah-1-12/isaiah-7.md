# Isaiah 7:14 - Immanuel, God with Us

## Isaiah 7:14

> 14. Therefore the Lord himself shall give you a sign; Behold, a virgin shall conceive, and bear a son, and shall call his name Immanuel.

[Read Isaiah 7](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/isa/7?lang=eng&id=p14#p14)

## A promise spoken into fear

Isaiah 7 begins with a frightened king and people facing a military threat. Read verses 1-9 before moving to verse 14. Isaiah's call to faith is addressed to people who have real reasons to be afraid.

Matthew applies this prophecy to Jesus Christ. Matthew 1:21-23 connects the promised birth with Jesus saving His people from their sins and explains Emmanuel as "God with us."

[Read Matthew 1:21-23](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/matt/1?lang=eng&id=p21-p23#p21)

Isaiah's original historical setting still matters. The 2026 Come, Follow Me study guide explains that prophecies can have more than one fulfillment. We do not need to settle every question about the immediate sign to Ahaz in order to study the New Testament's witness of Christ.

## Jesus Christ came to be our Savior

**Scriptural interpretation: Matthew 1:21-23.** Matthew directly quotes the prophecy and applies it to the birth of Jesus. This connection rests on scripture itself, not on an inferred conference citation.

Read Luke 1:31-33 alongside Isaiah 9:6-7 as a companion passage. Gabriel speaks of Jesus receiving David's throne and reigning without end. Together these passages turn our attention from a title alone to the identity and saving work of the promised Son.

[Read Luke 1:31-33](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/nt/luke/1?lang=eng&id=p31-p33#p31)

## God with us before the trouble is over

The video connects the call not to fear in Isaiah 7:4 with the name Immanuel in verse 14. The promise comes amid danger, not after life has become peaceful. In the chapter's setting, Syria and the northern kingdom of Israel threaten Judah. Matthew later identifies the birth of Jesus as a fulfillment of the Immanuel prophecy.

As a personal application, consider the difference between these two hopes: "Nothing difficult will happen" and "I will not have to face difficulty without God." The second does not make pain unimportant. It gives us someone to turn toward within it.

You might worry about choosing classes, a changing friendship, or an uncertain future. Trusting the Savior does not guarantee that every choice will be obvious or painless. It can mean praying, seeking wise help, and taking the next faithful step without knowing the whole outcome.

- Why might we want a complete plan more than the assurance of God's presence?
- What could seeking His help look like before you feel calm or certain?

## When the water feels high

Isaiah 8:7-8 pictures Assyria's advance as a river overflowing into Judah, reaching even to the neck. The passage ends by addressing Immanuel. The video reflects on the contrast between overwhelming danger and a name that means "God with us."

The flood describes a national crisis. As a personal comparison, it may also bring to mind times when problems feel almost too much to bear. Remembering Immanuel can become a prayer for help in those moments, not a demand that you stop feeling afraid. Asking a trusted adult or counselor for support can be part of that response.

## Questions to sit with

- What fears surround the promise in Isaiah 7? What does that add to our reading?
- How does "God with us" differ from a promise that nothing difficult will happen?
- What does Matthew tell us Jesus came to save us from?
- How could remembering the Savior's presence change the way we face one concern this week?
