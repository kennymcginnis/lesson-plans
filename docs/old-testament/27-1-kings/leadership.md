# 1 Kings 12:6-8 - Leadership That Serves

## A king asks for advice

Saul, David, and Solomon belong to the story of Israel's united monarchy. After Solomon's death, Rehoboam faces a request to lighten the burdens his father had placed on the people. The answer he gives will help shape the kingdom's future.

Read 1 Kings 12:1-8. The older advisers describe leadership in terms of service:

> "If thou wilt be a servant unto this people this day, and wilt serve them, and answer them, and speak good words to them, then they will be thy servants for ever."

1 Kings 12:7, KJV.

Verse 8 begins with a decisive contrast: Rehoboam forsakes that counsel. Read verses 10-16 to see the harsh response he chooses and the division that follows.

## What is a leader for?

Consider the difference between using people to preserve one's importance and using responsibility to help people flourish. A leader can model good choices, encourage growth, offer support, and remove unnecessary obstacles.

Service does not mean agreeing to every request or avoiding hard decisions. It means taking the needs of those affected seriously. Rehoboam asks for advice, but asking is not the same as being willing to listen.

The passage is not a rule that older people are always right and younger people always wrong. Examine the substance of the counsel: one answer offers service and good words; the other intensifies the burden.

## From a divided kingdom to Elijah

Rehoboam and Jeroboam are different people. Rehoboam is Solomon's son; Jeroboam becomes king over the northern tribes. The division and the northern kingdom's worship practices provide background for the later confrontation between Elijah and King Ahab.

By 1 Kings 17-19, the question of whom Israel will serve has become central. Elijah challenges Ahab and the people to turn from Baal to the Lord. Keep that larger question in view as we move into the individual stories.

## Questions to sit with

- Which words in verse 7 describe the kind of leadership you would trust?
- When have you been helped by someone who used their responsibility to remove a burden?
- How can we tell whether we want wise counsel or merely agreement?
- Where could we practice this kind of service in a family, calling, friendship, or workplace?
