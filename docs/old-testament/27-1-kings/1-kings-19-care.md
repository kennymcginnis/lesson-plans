# 1 Kings 19:1-8 - Care for the Weary

## Discouragement after a remarkable day

Chapter 18 ends with answered prayer and rain. Chapter 19 begins with Jezebel threatening Elijah's life. He flees, leaves his servant, and travels into the wilderness. Under a juniper tree, he tells the Lord that he has had enough and asks to die.

[Read 1 Kings 19:1-4](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/1-kgs/19?lang=eng&id=p1-p4#p1)

Do not flatten this into ordinary disappointment or diagnose Elijah from a few verses. The account portrays profound distress. A prophet who has seen the Lord's power still experiences fear, exhaustion, and loneliness.

Sometimes people assume that a recent success or strong spiritual experience should make them immune to discouragement. Elijah's story challenges that assumption. His need for help does not erase his faith or his service.

## Food and rest before the next journey

Elijah sleeps. An angel touches him and tells him to eat. He finds food and water, eats and drinks, and rests again. The angel returns:

> 7. "Arise and eat; because the journey is too great for thee."

1 Kings 19:7, KJV excerpt.

[Read 1 Kings 19:5-8](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/scriptures/ot/1-kgs/19?lang=eng&id=p5-p8#p5)

Notice the form of the help. Before the renewed assignment, Elijah receives care for his body. The acknowledgment that the journey is too great is not a dismissal of him. It explains why he needs nourishment.

Food and rest matter, but the passage is not a claim that they alone resolve serious emotional distress. Spiritual care, trusted relationships, and appropriate professional help can belong together.

## Be willing to bring help

One application is to ask who needs us to bring something sustaining. It might be a meal, a ride, help with a task, a listening ear, or company while they seek further support.

Do not dismiss an impulse to help because someone appears unusually capable or spiritually strong. Elijah was a prophet, and he still needed someone to attend to his needs.

Ask rather than assume what would help. Respect privacy, follow through on a specific offer, and return when care needs to continue. The angel comes twice; a single kind act may be a beginning rather than the whole response.

## Questions to sit with

- What assumptions about faithful people does Elijah's exhaustion challenge?
- What do you notice about the order of rest, food, and renewed direction?
- When has practical care helped you receive hope or keep going?
- Who might appreciate a specific, respectful offer of help this week?

Allow people to reflect without disclosing personal mental-health experiences. If someone says they may harm themselves, take it seriously and help them connect promptly with appropriate crisis or emergency support; do not treat a class discussion as sufficient care.
