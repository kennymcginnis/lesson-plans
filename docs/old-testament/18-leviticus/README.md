# Leviticus: The Way In

Main reading: Exodus 35-40; Leviticus 1; 4; 16; 19, with companion passages about holiness, offerings, and Jesus Christ.

> Will we stay outside the tent, or will we step in?

## Lesson notes

Exodus closes with God's glory filling the tabernacle and Moses unable to enter. Leviticus opens with the Lord calling to Moses from the tabernacle. Its instructions concern how a covenant people can live in the presence of a holy God.

The lesson follows four themes: being set apart, offering ourselves to God, receiving cleansing, and finding access to God through atonement. Read the ancient practices in their own setting, then turn to the New Testament's witness that Jesus Christ opens the way for us to approach God.

Holiness is not a claim that we are better than other people. It is belonging to the Lord and allowing that relationship to change our worship, choices, and care for others.

## A 25-minute class

1. **The way into God's presence, 3 minutes:** Read Exodus 40:34-35 and Leviticus 1:1. Introduce the question of how people can approach a holy God.
2. **Set apart and offered to God, 7 minutes:** Read Leviticus 19:2, 9-18. Discuss one way holiness shapes everyday treatment of others. Briefly connect willing, daily discipleship with Luke 9:23.
3. **Christ cleanses and opens the way, 10 minutes:** Choose Mark 1:40-42 or Mark 5:25-34, then read Leviticus 16:30 with Hebrews 9:24-28. Distinguish ritual impurity from wrongdoing and point to Christ's unique saving work.
4. **Draw near, 5 minutes:** Read Hebrews 4:16 and Romans 5:8. Allow a quiet moment to choose one response to God's grace, then close with your testimony of Jesus Christ.

Use the fuller offering and purity studies as preparation or follow-up reading. There is no need to explain every ritual in one class.

## Scriptures to emphasize

Prioritize Leviticus 19:2, 18; 16:30; Mark 1:40-42; Hebrews 9:24-28; and 4:16. Companion readings include Leviticus 1:4; 4:27-31; Exodus 29:38-39; John 10:17-18; Luke 9:23; and Mark 5:25-34.

## Reading the accounts carefully

Ritual impurity is not the same as moral guilt. Ordinary bodily processes and illness could affect ritual status without making a person wicked or less valuable. Nor are ancient Israel's dietary, clothing, and sacrificial regulations automatically instructions for Christians to reproduce today.

Hebrews explicitly interprets sanctuary and sacrifice in relation to Jesus Christ. Other comparisons in these notes are devotional applications, not claims that every detail of an ancient ceremony has one certain modern equivalent. Scripture excerpts use the King James Version unless otherwise identified.
